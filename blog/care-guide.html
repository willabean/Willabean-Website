How to clean a PVC-coated webbing dog collar, in roughly the time it takes to fill the kettle

The whole reason we use coated webbing is that it shrugs off the things leather can't. The care routine is honestly that short.

The two-minute version

Run the collar under a warm tap. Wipe the webbing with a soft cloth. Pat the metalwork dry. Hang it somewhere airy. That's the routine. You can stop reading here if you like, the rest is "what to do when your dog has done something especially memorable to it".

What PVC-coated webbing won't do

Unlike leather, it doesn't dry out and crack. Unlike nylon, it doesn't absorb pond water and start to smell. Unlike rope, it doesn't fray, knot up or hold on to mud. The whole material is engineered to be cleaned the way you'd clean a kitchen surface, with water and a wipe, not the way you'd clean a leather wallet.

The proper routine, after a bad walk

For the days where your dog comes home looking less like a dog and more like an art project. Four steps, none of them complicated:

1. Rinse off the worst

Take the collar off the dog. Run it under a warm tap, both sides. The coating is fully waterproof, so soak it as long as you need to flush mud, sand or grit out of the rivets, the buckle pin, and the underside of any layered seam.

2. Wipe the webbing

A soft cloth or kitchen sponge will do. For anything genuinely sticky (sap, beach tar, fox-roll residue, the lot), add a drop of washing-up liquid or unscented hand soap. Wipe it down, rinse it again to clear the soap, you're done.

3. Dry off the metalwork

The buckle, D-ring and rivets are solid metal with a corrosion-resistant finish, but standing water in the buckle pin will eventually dull the look. Pat them dry with a clean cloth. While you're there, work the buckle a few times to flush any water trapped inside.

4. Air-dry, don't bake

Hang it on a coat hook, the line in the airing cupboard, the back of a kitchen chair. Anywhere airy. Do not put it on a radiator, in a tumble drier, or somewhere it'll sit in midday sun for hours, prolonged heat can soften the coating over time. Room temperature, ten minutes, and it's ready for the next walk.

The deep-clean, every couple of months

If your dog's collar lives an active life, give it a proper clean every six to eight weeks: warm water, a drop of mild soap, a soft toothbrush around the rivets and the buckle. The toothbrush is the secret. It gets into the small gaps where mud actually accumulates, the underside of the layers and the seat of the rivets. Two minutes of brushing turns a tired-looking collar back into a new one.

What about the smell after a swim?

Sometimes a collar comes home from a swim smelling like the pond, and it's not the webbing that's holding the smell. It's a tiny pocket of stagnant water trapped behind the buckle pin or under the rivets. Run it under a tap, flex the buckle, dry it off. Smell gone. If the smell hangs around for more than a day, that's almost always a sign the dog rolled in something that wasn't water, and a soapy wipe sorts it.

What not to do

  • Don't put it in the washing machine. The metalwork batters around inside the drum, the hot wash cycle isn't kind to the coating, and you save zero time over a sink rinse.
  • Don't use bleach or industrial cleaners. The webbing surface is built to handle water, not chemistry. Bleach can discolour the coating.
  • Don't store it folded sharply or jammed in a hook for years. A loop or a flat lay is fine. A 90-degree fold left under pressure for a long time can crease the coating.
  • Don't ignore loose hardware. If a rivet works loose or the buckle starts to feel different, email us. We can fix it. The collar is built to last and we'd rather repair than replace.

How long should it last?

Properly looked after, a Willabean collar will run for years. PVC-coated webbing routinely outlasts leather collars by a wide margin, because it doesn't dry out, crack, or absorb the things that age leather. The metalwork has a corrosion-resistant finish, which is what makes the difference on dogs that swim regularly.

We've got customers whose Hugos are five years deep and still in regular use. The webbing looks the same, the metalwork's still working, the colour's still bright. That's the point.
